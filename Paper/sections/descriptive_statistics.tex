\section{Descriptive Statistics}

The descriptive section is organized around the empirical facts that motivate the
regression analysis. We first define the working sample, then document where
workers are located in the firm-size distribution, how wages vary across that
distribution, and how much of the raw pattern is related to formality and other
observable wage gaps.

%Table~\ref{tab:sample-construction} defines the working sample and period coverage. The sample contains about 3.85 million worker-year observations with valid wages, firm-size information, worker controls, and expansion weights over 17 survey years. Detailed employment, wage, and worker-composition tables are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional-descriptives}; the main text keeps the focus on the exhibits that structure the interpretation.

Figure~\ref{fig:income-mean-comparison} establishes the first raw fact behind the paper: mean real hourly income rises sharply with firm size. The gradient is visible in both endpoint years. In 2008, solo workers earned 6,100 COL 2025 pesos per hour on average, compared with 13,369 COL 2025 pesos per hour among workers in firms with 101 or more employees, a ratio of 2.2 to 1. By 2025, the corresponding means were 8,351 and 18,502 COL 2025 pesos per hour, again a ratio of roughly 2.2 to 1. %The middle categories also sit well above solo work in 2025: workers in firms with 6--10 employees earned 10,609 COL 2025 pesos per hour, and those in firms with 51--100 employees earned 14,481 COL 2025 pesos per hour.
The bubble sizes show that these are not comparisons among negligible cells: solo work accounted for 28.9 percent of employment in 2025, while the 101+ category accounted for 31.9 percent.

Figure~\ref{fig:employment-share-comparison} establishes the second raw fact: employment is heavily concentrated at the bottom of the firm-size distribution. In 2025, solo workers accounted for 28.9 percent of employment, firms with 2--3 workers for 9.8 percent, firms with 4--5 workers for 6.2 percent, and firms with 6--10 workers for 6.3 percent. Taken together, solo work and firms with up to ten workers represented 51.2 percent of employment. By contrast, the middle of the distribution was thin: firms with 11--100 workers jointly accounted for 16.9 percent. The largest category grew substantially, from 22.2 percent of employment in 2008 to 31.9 percent in 2025, but this increase did not eliminate the large employment mass in solo work and micro-firms, which still covered about half of workers in 2025.

Figure~\ref{fig:wage-gaps-2025} puts the firm-size gradient in context by comparing it with other raw remuneration gaps in 2025. The gender gap is small in these weighted means: men earn 1.6 percent above the overall mean and women 1.9 percent below it. The formal-informal gap is much larger. Formal workers earn 31.0 percent above the overall mean, while informal workers earn 41.5 percent below it; the formal-informal wage ratio is therefore about 2.2 to 1, close to the 2.2-to-1 ratio between 101+ firms and solo work in Figure~\ref{fig:income-mean-comparison}. Education and sector gaps are also large: workers with higher education earn 51.9 percent above the overall mean, while workers with preschool or less earn 64.4 percent below it, more than a 4-to-1 ratio. Across activities, household employers are 43.2 percent below the mean, whereas mining is 142.6 percent above it. The raw firm-size gap is therefore far larger than the gender gap, comparable to the formality gap, and smaller than the largest education and sectoral gaps. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:descriptive-wage-gaps-2025} reports the corresponding means, employment shares, and gaps across sex, education, and economic activities.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:descriptive-wage-groups-mean-2008-2025} reports mean real hourly wages in 2008 and 2025 for all workers, by sex, and by formality status. The table gives the exact magnitudes behind Figure~\ref{fig:income-mean-comparison}. Three patterns stand out. First, the raw firm-size gradient was already large in 2008 and remained large in 2025: mean hourly income in the 101+ category was about 2.2 times the solo-worker mean in both endpoint years. Second, the gender gap in the aggregate means is small relative to the cross-firm-size gradient. Third, the formal-informal wage gap is large in both years, making formality a central part of the interpretation. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:descriptive-wage-growth-2008-2025} reports the corresponding wage changes between 2008 and 2025.

Figure~\ref{fig:income-formality-comparison} looks at the firm-size wage premium by formality status. The bubble size represents the percentage of formal or
informal workers in each firm-size category in the corresponding year. Formal workers earn more than informal workers and are much more concentrated in large
firms. The wage gradient by firm size is still visible within informal workers. Among informal workers, mean hourly income rises from 6,894 pesos for solo workers to 15,843 pesos in firms with 101 or more workers in 2025. Among formal workers, the profile is closer to a U-shape: formal solo workers earned 17,217 pesos per hour in 2025, formal workers in firms with 4--5 workers earned 13,692 pesos, and formal workers in firms with 101 or more workers earned 18,548 pesos. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:formal-worker-profile-2025} shows that formal solo work is a selected but heterogeneous category: 83.0 percent are own-account workers and 53.7 percent have higher education, with a sizable concentration in professional and administrative services, but 15.8 percent are domestic workers and 15.7 percent are in transport. Thus, the raw U-shape should be interpreted as evidence of worker composition within formal solo work, rather than as evidence that all formal solo workers are high-earning professionals.

Figure~\ref{fig:income-sex-comparison} shows that the raw firm-size gradient is
similar for men and women. In 2025, male solo workers earned 8,749 pesos per
hour, compared with 18,869 pesos in firms with 101 or more workers; for women,
the corresponding means were 7,889 and 18,096 pesos. The large-firm advantage is
therefore visible within both sexes. The figure also shows that the gender gap
within a given firm-size category is small relative to the gap between solo work
and large-firm employment.

Figure~\ref{fig:income-education-comparison} shows two facts regarding the relationship between education and firm size. First, workers with preschool or less, primary, and secondary education were much more concentrated in solo work than workers with higher education. In 2025, solo work accounted for 67.7 percent of employment among workers with preschool or less and 55.1 percent among workers with primary education, compared with only 16.4 percent among workers with higher education. Conversely, the 101+ category accounted for 48.7 percent of workers with higher education but only 2.6 percent of workers with preschool or less and 7.1 percent of workers with primary education. Second, there is still a firm-size wage premium within each education group. The ratio between mean hourly income in 101+ firms and solo work was 2.0 among workers with preschool or less, 1.7 among workers with primary education, 1.3 among workers with secondary education, and 1.6 among workers with higher education.

Figure~\ref{fig:income-sector-comparison} shows that the firm-size wage premium is widespread across economic activities. Excluding extraterritorial organizations, mean hourly income in firms with 101 or more workers exceeded solo-worker income in 14 of 16 sectors in 2008 and 15 of 16 sectors in 2025. The two 2008 exceptions were finance and insurance and business services, where solo workers earned slightly more than workers in the largest firms; in 2025, the remaining exception is households as employers. The gradient is especially pronounced in mining, utilities and water, manufacturing, accommodation and food, agriculture, and transport, while it is much flatter in information and communication, business services, finance, and health. These sector panels therefore reinforce the aggregate pattern while also showing that the size premium varies substantially across activities and is sometimes nonmonotonic.

Taken together, the descriptive evidence motivates the regression analysis
below. The central pattern is not only that the aggregate wage gradient rises
with firm size. The figures also provide suggestive evidence that a firm-size
wage premium appears within each sex, within each education group, in most
economic activities, and even within both formal and informal employment. At the
same time, the descriptive results show strong sorting by formality, education,
sector, and position in the firm-size distribution. The econometric exercise
therefore asks how much of the raw firm-size premium survives after controlling
for observable worker characteristics, formality, and sector-year conditions.
