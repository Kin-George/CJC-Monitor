\section{Introduction}

Large firms tend to pay higher wages than small firms. This empirical regularity, usually known as the firm-size wage premium, is one of the classic facts in labor economics. Its interpretation, however, is not mechanical. The premium may reflect worker sorting, monitoring costs, efficiency wages, rent sharing, or productivity differences across firms \citep{brown1989employer,oi1999firm,troske1999evidence}. The matched employer-employee literature further shows that wages reflect both worker heterogeneity and firm wage-setting heterogeneity \citep{abowd1999high,card2013workplace,song2019firming}.

The firm-size wage premium has implications not only for wage inequality but
also for allocative efficiency, and the connection between the two depends on
what the premium reflects. An older reading, anchored by panel evidence from
\citet{SoderbomTealWambugu2005} for Kenya and Ghana, attributed much of the
cross-sectional firm-size premium in developing countries to unobserved worker
quality rather than firm-side pay setting; under that reading, reallocating
workers across firm sizes would not necessarily raise aggregate output. More
recent matched employer--employee work has shifted the interpretation. Mover
designs in the tradition of \citet{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999} and
\citet{CardHeiningKline2013} find that firm-specific pay components survive
controls for worker fixed effects---in the United States \citep{Song2019}, in
Brazil \citep{AlvarezBenguriaEngbomMoser2018, GerardLagosSeverniniCard2021,
EngbomMoser2022}, and in Costa Rica
\citep{AlfaroUrenaManeliciVasquez2022}. These studies show that worker sorting
is not the whole story. To the extent that firm pay components may reflect
productivity differences across firms---through management practices, capital
intensity, or technology \citep{Bloom2019}---and are transmitted to workers
through rent sharing \citep{Card2022}, the allocation of employment across firm
size categories may matter for aggregate productivity. Our data do not allow us
to estimate firm-specific pay components or marginal products directly. We
therefore use the firm-size wage premium as a diagnostic statistic rather than
as a direct test of misallocation. The relevant empirical object is not only the slope of wages
with respect to firm size, but also the mass of workers located at each point of
that distribution: a steep premium is more consequential in a labor market where
most employment is concentrated in solo work and micro-firms than in one where
small firms represent a thin margin.

Colombia is a natural setting in which to study this question. The country
combines a highly heterogeneous productive structure with a large informal
sector, strong differences between small and large firms, and persistent wage
inequality. Existing Colombian work has studied wage differentials by sector and
firm characteristics in the formal labor market \citep{iregui2012diferenciales},
but there is little systematic evidence on the firm-size wage premium using
nationally representative household survey data over a long period. This gap
matters because firm size is closely connected to formality, access to
technology, managerial capacity, regulatory compliance, and the allocation of
workers across the productive structure. A central empirical challenge is
therefore to distinguish a firm-size gradient from the formal-informal wage gap,
and to ask whether the size gradient itself differs within formal and informal
employment.

This motivation is closely related to the recent policy diagnosis in
\citet{eslava2026crecimiento}. They argue that growth and equity in Colombia are
not competing objectives, but joint outcomes of productive transformation. In
their diagnosis, low-income households are disproportionately linked to
own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and jobs with low earnings,
whereas higher-income households are more likely to be connected to larger and
more formal employers. The implication is that the organization of production is
itself a mechanism through which inequality and low aggregate productivity are
jointly reproduced. Our paper provides microeconometric evidence on one central
component of that mechanism: the wage gradient associated with firm size.

This paper documents the firm-size wage premium in Colombia using harmonized
microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and
2021--2025. The central questions are descriptive and econometric: do workers in
larger firms earn higher real hourly labor income than comparable workers in solo
work or smaller firms? How much of the raw gradient remains after accounting for
observable worker characteristics, formality, and sector-year conditions? Has
the premium widened or narrowed over time? Does the relationship between
firm size and wages differ between formal and informal workers? The resulting
estimates are conditional wage gaps, not causal effects of firm size. They are
nevertheless informative because persistent conditional gaps in a labor market
with concentrated micro-firm employment are a useful diagnostic of segmentation
and possible allocative frictions.

We document five main facts. First, employment is concentrated at the bottom of
the firm-size distribution: solo workers and firms with up to ten workers account
for about two thirds of employment. Second, the raw wage gradient by firm size is
large and widespread across sectors, education groups, gender, and formality
status. Third, the gradient attenuates substantially after adding worker
controls, sector-year fixed effects, and especially formality, but it does not
disappear: in the full specification, workers in firms with 101 or more
employees earn about 43 percent more than otherwise comparable solo workers.
Fourth, the conditional premium is positive throughout the period, with no
statistically conclusive evidence of either a secular decline or a systematic
widening. Fifth, formality matters in two distinct ways. Formal workers earn
more than informal workers within every firm-size category, but the conditional
firm-size gradient is much steeper among informal workers than among formal
workers. If the size premium is interpreted as a marker of allocative frictions
rather than as pure sorting, those frictions appear most clearly in the informal
segment.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 describes the GEIH data,
the harmonization of firm-size categories, and the construction of real hourly
labor income. Section~3 presents the descriptive evidence on employment shares
and wage gaps by firm size, formality, education, gender, sector, and year.
Section~4 introduces the econometric specifications. Section~5 reports the main
regression results, the dynamic estimates, and the formality interactions.
Section~6 discusses interpretation and limitations, Section~7 draws policy
implications, and Section~8 concludes.
