\section{Introduction}

Large firms tend to pay higher wages than small firms. This empirical regularity, usually known as the firm-size wage premium, is one of the classic facts in labor economics. Its interpretation, however, is not mechanical. The premium may reflect worker sorting, monitoring costs, efficiency wages, rent sharing, or productivity differences across firms \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. The matched employer--employee literature helps discipline this ambiguity by distinguishing persistent worker components from employer-associated pay components, although the latter may reflect productivity, rents, wage-setting policies, or other firm attributes \citep{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,Song2019}.

The firm-size wage premium has implications not only for wage inequality but
also for allocative efficiency, and the connection between the two depends on
what the premium reflects. If the premium only captures unobserved worker
quality or compensating differentials, moving workers across firm sizes would
not necessarily raise aggregate output. If instead part of the premium reflects
firm-side pay differences tied to productivity or rents, the allocation of
employment across firm-size categories may matter for productivity as well as
for inequality. Evidence from developing economies already warns against
reducing the premium to sorting alone: \citet{SoderbomTealWambugu2005} show for
Kenya and Ghana that the firm-size effect remains substantial after controlling
for individual fixed effects, while \citet{reed2019large} document large
premiums across developing economies, with cross-country variation in the role
of human-capital sorting. Matched employer--employee studies in the tradition of
\citet{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999} and \citet{CardHeiningKline2013} reinforce
this interpretation by showing that employer-side wage setting and firm policies
matter for wages and inequality in France, Germany, the United States
\citep{Song2019}, and Brazil
\citep{AlvarezBenguriaEngbomMoser2018,GerardLagosSeverniniCard2021,
EngbomMoser2022}. Taken together, this evidence suggests that worker sorting is
not the whole story. To the extent that employer pay components reflect
productivity-linked firm attributes and are transmitted to workers through rent
sharing or firm-level wage setting, the allocation of employment across
firm-size categories may matter for aggregate productivity. The relevant
empirical object is therefore not only the slope of wages with respect to firm
size, but also the distribution of workers across firm sizes, since a steep
premium is more consequential in a labor market where most employment is
concentrated in solo work and micro-firms than in one where small firms
represent a thin margin.

In Colombia, the motivation is both empirical and policy-oriented. A recent
policy diagnosis argues that low-income households are disproportionately linked
to own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and low-earning jobs,
whereas higher-income households are more often connected to larger and more
formal employers \citep{eslava2026crecimiento}. This diagnosis is suggestive,
but the observed association between firm size and household income may reflect
worker sorting or formality rather than firm scale itself. Nonetheless, the wage
gradient associated with firm size has not been systematically documented in
Colombia. Existing work has studied wage differentials by sector and firm
characteristics in the formal labor market \citep{iregui2012diferenciales}, but
less is known about the relationship between firm size and wages once solo work,
micro-firms, and informal employment are included. The central empirical
challenge is therefore to document the firm-size wage gradient while
distinguishing it from worker characteristics and the formal-informal wage gap.

This paper documents the firm-size wage premium in Colombia using harmonized microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. The central questions are descriptive and econometric: do workers in larger firms earn higher real hourly labor income than comparable workers in solo work or smaller firms? How much of the raw gradient remains after accounting for observable worker characteristics, formality, and sector-year conditions? Has the premium widened or narrowed over time? Does the relationship between firm size and wages differ between formal and informal workers? The GEIH does not identify employers or observe marginal products, so the resulting estimates are conditional wage gaps, not causal effects of firm size. They are nevertheless informative because persistent conditional gaps in a labor market with concentrated micro-firm employment are a useful diagnostic of segmentation and possible allocative frictions.

We document five main facts. First, employment is concentrated at the bottom of the firm-size distribution: solo workers and firms with up to ten workers account for about two thirds of employment. Second, the raw wage gradient by firm size is large and widespread across sectors, education groups, gender, and formality status. Third, the gradient attenuates substantially after adding worker controls, sector-year fixed effects, and especially formality, but it does not disappear: in the full specification, workers in firms with 101 or more employees earn about 43 percent more than otherwise comparable solo workers. Fourth, the conditional premium is positive throughout the period, with no statistically conclusive evidence of either a secular decline or a systematic widening. Fifth, formality matters in two distinct ways. Formal workers earn more than informal workers within every firm-size category, but the conditional firm-size gradient is much steeper among informal workers than among formal workers. If the size premium is interpreted as a marker of allocative frictions rather than as pure sorting, those frictions appear most clearly in the informal sector.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 describes the GEIH data, the harmonization of firm-size categories, and the construction of real hourly labor income. Section~3 presents the descriptive evidence on employment shares and wage gaps by firm size, formality, education, gender, sector, and year. Section~4 introduces the econometric specifications. Section~5 reports the main regression results, the dynamic estimates, and the formality interactions. Section~6 discusses interpretation and limitations, Section~7 draws policy implications, and Section~8 concludes.
