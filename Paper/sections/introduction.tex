\section{Introduction}

Firm size is one of the most visible dimensions of a country's productive structure. In labor markets where many workers are own-account workers or employed in micro-firms, the wage difference between small and large firms is a clue about potential gains from scaling up firms. This paper asks whether Colombian workers in larger firms earn higher real hourly wages than comparable workers in smaller firms, how much of that gap is explained by observable worker characteristics, and whether the wage gap between small and large firms differs between formal and informal employment.

The firm-size wage premium is a classic empirical regularity: large firms tend to pay higher wages than small firms, and this pattern often remains after conditioning on observed worker characteristics \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. A conditional premium, however, must be interpreted carefully. It means that workers in larger firms earn more than observably similar workers in smaller firms. It does not prove that moving a given worker to a larger firm would raise aggregate output, since unobserved worker quality or other omitted factors may still matter. At the same time, the persistence of the premium after controlling for observables is informative: it is consistent with firm-side wage differences, labor-market segmentation, or allocative frictions, even if it is not direct proof of any one mechanism.

The literature supports this cautious interpretation. Evidence from developing economies shows that firm-size effects often survive stronger controls for worker heterogeneity: \citet{SoderbomTealWambugu2005} find substantial firm-size effects after controlling for individual fixed effects in Kenya and Ghana, and \citet{reed2019large} documents large premiums across developing economies, with cross-country variation in the role of human-capital sorting. Matched employer--employee studies further show that employer-associated pay components matter for wages and inequality, while also making clear that those components may reflect productivity, rents, wage-setting policies, or other firm attributes rather than productivity alone \citep{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,Song2019, AlvarezBenguriaEngbomMoser2018,GerardLagosSeverniniCard2021,EngbomMoser2022}. The firm-size wage premium is therefore a diagnostic statistic rather than a causal estimate.

Our motivation to measure the firm-size wage premium in Colombia is both empirical and policy-oriented. A recent policy diagnosis argues that low-income households are disproportionately linked to own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and low-earning jobs, whereas higher-income households are more often connected to larger and more formal employers \citep{eslava2026crecimiento}. This diagnosis is suggestive, but the observed association between firm size and household income may reflect worker sorting or formality rather than firm scale itself. Nonetheless, the wage gradient
associated with firm size has not been systematically documented in Colombia. Existing work has studied wage differentials by sector and firm characteristics
in the formal labor market \citep{iregui2012diferenciales}, but less is known about the relationship between firm size and wages once solo work, micro-firms, and informal employment are included.

We study this question using harmonized microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. We construct a comparable firm-size classification, measure real hourly labor income, document the distribution of employment across firm sizes, and estimate wage gradients that progressively condition on sector-year effects, worker characteristics, and formality. Because the GEIH does not identify employers or observe marginal products, the estimates are conditional wage gaps rather than causal effects of firm size. Their interpretation depends on what the remaining premium reflects, but their economic importance also depends on where workers are located in the firm-size distribution.

The evidence delivers five main results. First, employment is concentrated at the bottom of the firm-size distribution: solo workers and firms with up to ten workers account for about two thirds of employment. Second, the raw wage gradient by firm size is large and widespread across sectors, education groups, and formality status, but not across genders. Third, the gradient attenuates substantially after adding worker controls, sector-year fixed effects, and especially formality, but it does not disappear: in the full specification, workers in firms with 101 or more employees earn about 43 percent more than otherwise comparable solo workers. Fourth, the conditional premium is positive throughout the period, with no statistically conclusive evidence of either a secular decline or a systematic widening. Fifth, the formality status affects the firm-size wage premium. Formal workers earn more than informal workers within every firm-size category, but the conditional firm-size wage premium is much steeper among informal workers. Within formal employment, the broad size gradient is weak relative to formal solo workers, although workers in 101+ firms earn more than workers in intermediate-size formal firms. If the wage gaps are interpreted as diagnostics of allocative frictions rather than as pure sorting, the evidence points to three possible margins for efficiency gains: movement from informal to formal
employment, scaling of informal micro-units, and, more selectively, movement or growth within the formal sector toward larger employers.
