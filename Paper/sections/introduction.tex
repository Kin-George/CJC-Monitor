\section{Introduction}

Firm size is a well-established correlate of wages in labor economics. In labor markets where many workers are own-account workers or employed in micro-firms, the wage difference between small and large firms is a clue about the potential gains from either scaling up productive small firms or reallocating workers toward larger, more productive firms. This paper asks whether Colombian workers in larger firms earn higher real hourly wages than observably similar workers in smaller firms, how much of that gap is explained by observable worker characteristics, whether the gap has increased or decreased over time, and whether the wage gap between small and large firms differs between formal and informal employment.

The firm-size wage premium is a classic empirical regularity: large firms tend to pay higher wages than small firms, and this pattern often remains after conditioning on observed worker characteristics \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. A conditional premium, however, must be interpreted carefully. It means that workers in larger firms earn more than observably similar workers in smaller firms. It does not prove that moving a given worker to a larger firm would raise aggregate output, since unobserved worker quality or other omitted factors may still matter. At the same time, the persistence of the premium after controlling for observables is informative: it is consistent with firm-side wage differences, labor-market segmentation, or allocative frictions, even if it is not direct proof of any one mechanism.

The literature supports this cautious interpretation. Evidence from developing economies shows that firm-size effects often survive stronger controls for worker heterogeneity: \citet{SoderbomTealWambugu2005} find substantial firm-size effects after controlling for individual fixed effects in Kenya and Ghana, and \citet{reed2019large} documents large premiums across developing economies, with cross-country variation in the role of human-capital sorting. Matched employer--employee studies further show that employer-associated pay components matter for wages and inequality, while also making clear that those components may reflect wage-setting policies, rent sharing, or other firm attributes rather than productivity alone \citep{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,Song2019,AlvarezBenguriaEngbomMoser2018,GerardLagosSeverniniCard2021,EngbomMoser2022}. The firm-size wage premium is therefore a diagnostic statistic rather than a causal estimate. Its relevance is greater in settings where employment remains concentrated away from the right tail of the firm-size distribution, since recent growth theory links the rise of large firms to economic development \citep{Chen2026}.

Our motivation to measure the firm-size wage premium in Colombia is both empirical and policy-oriented. A recent policy diagnosis argues that low-income households are disproportionately linked to own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and low-earning jobs, whereas higher-income households are more often connected to larger and more formal employers \citep{eslava2026crecimiento}. This diagnosis is suggestive, but it does not show whether firm size itself is the cause of higher wages. The observed link between larger employers and higher household income may instead reflect the fact that larger firms employ workers with different characteristics, or that larger firms are more likely to offer formal jobs. This distinction matters because the wage gradient associated with firm size has not been systematically documented in Colombia. Existing work has studied wage differentials by sector and firm characteristics in the formal labor market \citep{iregui2012diferenciales}, but less is known about the relationship between firm size and wages once solo work, micro-firms, and informal employment are included.

This focus on formality is motivated by evidence that firm size and informality are closely linked. \citet{ElBadaouiStroblWalsh2010} show that what appears to be a formal-sector wage premium can largely reflect firm-size wage differences, while \citet{GalianiWeinschelbaum2012} model informality as the joint outcome of worker and firm choices, with small firms more likely to operate informally. For Colombia, \citet{DazaGamboa2013} document persistent formal-informal wage gaps, and recent evidence on microfirms shows that business registration and labor-regulation compliance are associated with productivity differences \citep{GutierrezRodriguezLesmes2023}. These findings motivate our focus on the interaction between firm size and labor formality: the empirical question is not only whether larger firms pay more, but whether the size gradient remains once the formal-informal margin is made explicit.

We study this question using harmonized microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. We construct a comparable firm-size classification, measure real hourly labor income, document the distribution of employment across firm sizes, and estimate wage gradients that progressively condition on sector-year effects, worker characteristics, and formality. Because the GEIH does not identify employers or observe marginal products, the estimates are conditional wage gaps rather than causal effects of firm size. Still, these gaps are informative for inequality and efficiency because they show how wages vary across the firm-size distribution in a labor market where employment is heavily concentrated in solo work and micro-firms.

The evidence delivers five main results. First, employment is concentrated at the bottom of the firm-size distribution: solo workers and firms with up to ten workers account for about two thirds of employment. Second, the raw wage gradient by firm size is large and widespread between sectors, education groups, and formality status, but not between genders. Third, the gradient attenuates substantially after adding worker controls, sector-year fixed effects and formality, but it does not disappear: in the full specification, workers in firms with 101 or more employees earn about 43 percent more than otherwise comparable solo workers. Fourth, the conditional premium is positive throughout the period, with no statistically conclusive evidence of either a secular decline or a systematic widening. Fifth, the formality status affects the firm-size wage premium. Formal workers earn more than informal workers within every firm-size category, but the conditional firm-size wage premium is much steeper among informal workers. Within formal employment, the broad size gradient is weak relative to formal solo workers, although workers in 101+ firms earn more than workers in intermediate-size formal firms. If the wage gaps are interpreted as diagnostics of allocative frictions rather than as pure sorting, the evidence points to three potential margins for efficiency gains: movement from informal to formal employment, scaling of informal micro-units, and, more selectively, movement or growth within the formal sector toward larger employers.

It has studied wage inequality, trade reforms, informality, payroll taxes, and formal-informal wage gaps \citep{AttanasioGoldbergPavcnik2004,MondragonPenaWills2010,KuglerKugler2009,DazaGamboa2013}. A smaller set of studies relates firm characteristics to wages or productivity. \citet{TanBatra1997} study technology and firm-size wage differentials using firm-level data that include Colombia; \citet{iregui2012diferenciales} analyze wage differentials in legally constituted firms with at least ten workers; and \citet{FlorezMoralesMedinaLobo2017} document labor flows across firm-size and wage categories using employer-employee records. Related manufacturing evidence links plant size to productivity, quality, and reallocation \citep{KuglerVerhoogen2012,EslavaHaltiwangerPinzon2019,CamachoConoverScrimgeour2024}. Our contribution is to put firm size itself at the center of the wage analysis in nationally representative household data, covering solo work, micro-firms, informal employment, and the 2008--2025 period.
