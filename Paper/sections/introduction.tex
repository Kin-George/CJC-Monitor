\section{Introduction}

Large firms tend to pay higher wages than small firms. This empirical regularity, usually known as the firm-size wage premium, is one of the classic facts in labor economics. Its interpretation, however, is not mechanical. The premium may reflect worker sorting, monitoring costs, efficiency wages, rent sharing, or productivity differences across firms \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. The matched employer-employee literature further shows that wages reflect both worker heterogeneity and firm wage-setting heterogeneity \citep{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,Song2019}.

The firm-size wage premium has implications not only for wage inequality but
also for allocative efficiency, and the connection between the two depends on
what the premium reflects. If the premium only captures unobserved worker
quality or compensating differentials, moving workers across firm sizes would
not necessarily raise aggregate output. If instead part of the premium reflects
firm-side pay differences tied to productivity or rents, the allocation of
employment across firm size categories may matter for productivity as well as
for inequality. Evidence from developing economies already warns against
reducing the premium to sorting alone: \citet{SoderbomTealWambugu2005} show for
Kenya and Ghana that the firm-size effect remains substantial after controlling
for individual fixed effects, while \citet{reed2019large} document large
premiums across developing economies, with cross-country variation in the role
of human-capital sorting. Recent matched employer--employee work reinforces this
interpretation. Designs in the tradition of
\citet{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999} and \citet{CardHeiningKline2013} show that
employer-side wage setting and firm policies matter for wages and inequality in France, Germany, the
United States \citep{Song2019}, and Brazil
\citep{AlvarezBenguriaEngbomMoser2018,GerardLagosSeverniniCard2021,
EngbomMoser2022}. Related firm-to-firm evidence from Costa Rica shows that
joining multinational supply chains raises domestic firm performance and
employment
\citep{AlfaroUrenaManeliciVasquez2022}. These studies show that worker sorting
is not the whole story. To the extent that employer pay components reflect
productivity-linked firm attributes, including management practices
\citep{Bloom2019}, and are transmitted to workers through rent sharing or
firm-level wage setting \citep{Card2022}, the allocation of employment across
firm size categories may matter for aggregate productivity. Our data do not
allow us to estimate firm-specific pay components or marginal products directly.
We therefore use the firm-size wage premium as a diagnostic statistic rather
than as a direct test of misallocation. The relevant empirical object is not only the slope of wages
with respect to firm size, but also the mass of workers located at each point of
that distribution: a steep premium is more consequential in a labor market where
most employment is concentrated in solo work and micro-firms than in one where
small firms represent a thin margin.

Colombia is a natural setting in which to study this question. The country
combines a highly heterogeneous productive structure with a large informal
sector, strong differences between small and large firms, and persistent wage
inequality. Existing Colombian work has studied wage differentials by sector and
firm characteristics in the formal labor market \citep{iregui2012diferenciales},
but there is little systematic evidence on the firm-size wage premium using
nationally representative household survey data over a long period. This gap
matters because firm size is closely connected to formality, access to
technology, managerial capacity, regulatory compliance, and the allocation of
workers across the productive structure. A central empirical challenge is
therefore to distinguish a firm-size gradient from the formal-informal wage gap,
and to ask whether the size gradient itself differs within formal and informal
employment.

This motivation is closely related to the recent policy diagnosis in
\citet{eslava2026crecimiento}. They argue that growth and equity in Colombia are
not competing objectives, but joint outcomes of productive transformation. In
their diagnosis, low-income households are disproportionately linked to
own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and jobs with low earnings,
whereas higher-income households are more likely to be connected to larger and
more formal employers. The implication is that the organization of production is
itself a mechanism through which inequality and low aggregate productivity are
jointly reproduced. Our paper provides microeconometric evidence on one central
component of that mechanism: the wage gradient associated with firm size.

This paper documents the firm-size wage premium in Colombia using harmonized
microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and
2021--2025. The central questions are descriptive and econometric: do workers in
larger firms earn higher real hourly labor income than comparable workers in solo
work or smaller firms? How much of the raw gradient remains after accounting for
observable worker characteristics, formality, and sector-year conditions? Has
the premium widened or narrowed over time? Does the relationship between
firm size and wages differ between formal and informal workers? The resulting
estimates are conditional wage gaps, not causal effects of firm size. They are
nevertheless informative because persistent conditional gaps in a labor market
with concentrated micro-firm employment are a useful diagnostic of segmentation
and possible allocative frictions.

We document five main facts. First, employment is concentrated at the bottom of the firm-size distribution: solo workers and firms with up to ten workers account for about two thirds of employment. Second, the raw wage gradient by firm size is large and widespread across sectors, education groups, gender, and formality status. Third, the gradient attenuates substantially after adding worker controls, sector-year fixed effects, and especially formality, but it does not disappear: in the full specification, workers in firms with 101 or more employees earn about 43 percent more than otherwise comparable solo workers. Fourth, the conditional premium is positive throughout the period, with no statistically conclusive evidence of either a secular decline or a systematic widening. Fifth, formality matters in two distinct ways. Formal workers earn more than informal workers within every firm-size category, but the conditional firm-size gradient is much steeper among informal workers than among formal workers. If the size premium is interpreted as a marker of allocative frictions rather than as pure sorting, those frictions appear most clearly in the informal sector.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 describes the GEIH data, the harmonization of firm-size categories, and the construction of real hourly labor income. Section~3 presents the descriptive evidence on employment shares and wage gaps by firm size, formality, education, gender, sector, and year. Section~4 introduces the econometric specifications. Section~5 reports the main regression results, the dynamic estimates, and the formality interactions. Section~6 discusses interpretation and limitations, Section~7 draws policy implications, and Section~8 concludes.
