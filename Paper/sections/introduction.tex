\section{Introduction}

Firm size is a well-established correlate of wages in labor economics. In labor markets where many workers are own-account workers or employed in micro-firms, wage differences between small and large firms are a clue about labor-market segmentation and about where potential gains from firm growth or worker reallocation might lie. This paper asks whether Colombian workers in larger firms earn higher real hourly wages than observably similar workers in smaller firms, how much of that gap is explained by observable worker characteristics, whether the gap has increased or decreased over time, and whether the wage gap between small and large firms differs between formal and informal employment.

Our motivation to measure the firm-size wage premium in Colombia is both empirical and policy-oriented. A recent policy diagnosis argues that low-income households are disproportionately linked to own-account work, very small businesses, informality, and low-earning jobs, whereas higher-income households are more often connected to larger and more formal employers \citep{eslava2026crecimiento}. This diagnosis is suggestive, but it does not show whether firm size remains associated with higher wages after accounting for worker characteristics and formality. The observed link between larger employers and higher household income may instead reflect the fact that larger firms employ workers with different characteristics, or that larger firms are more likely to offer formal jobs. This distinction matters because the wage gradient associated with firm size has not been systematically documented in Colombia. Its relevance is greater in settings like Colombia where employment remains concentrated away from the right tail of the firm-size distribution, since recent growth theory links the rise of large firms to economic development \citep{Chen2026}.

We study this question using harmonized microdata from the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH) for 2008--2019 and 2021--2025. We construct a comparable firm-size classification, measure real hourly labor income, document the distribution of employment across firm sizes, and estimate wage gradients that progressively condition on sector-year effects, worker characteristics, and formality. Because the GEIH does not identify employers or observe marginal products, the estimates are conditional wage gaps rather than causal effects of firm size. Still, these gaps are informative for wage inequality and for discussing possible allocative frictions, especially because employment is heavily concentrated in solo work and micro-firms.

The evidence delivers five main results. First, employment is concentrated at the bottom of the firm-size distribution: solo workers and firms with up to ten workers account for about two thirds of employment. Second, the raw wage gradient by firm size is large and widespread between sectors, education groups, and formality status, but not between genders. Third, the gradient attenuates substantially after adding worker controls, sector-year fixed effects and formality, but it does not disappear: in the full specification, workers in firms with 101 or more employees earn about 43 percent more than otherwise comparable solo workers. Fourth, the conditional premium is positive throughout the period, with no statistically conclusive evidence of either a secular decline or a systematic widening. Fifth, formality status affects the firm-size wage premium. Formal workers earn more than informal workers within every firm-size category, but the conditional firm-size wage premium is much steeper among informal workers. Within formal employment, the broad size gradient is weak relative to formal solo workers, although workers in 101+ firms earn more than workers in intermediate-size formal firms. If the wage gaps are interpreted as diagnostics of allocative frictions rather than as pure sorting, the evidence points to three potential margins for efficiency gains: movement from informal to formal employment, scaling of informal micro-units, and, more selectively, movement or growth within the formal sector from intermediate-size firms toward larger employers.

\subsection{Related Literature}

The firm-size wage premium is a classic empirical regularity: large firms tend to pay higher wages than small firms, and this pattern often remains after conditioning on observed worker characteristics. The persistence of the premium after observable controls means that measured worker characteristics and sector composition do not fully account for wage differences between small and large firms. The literature interprets this residual cautiously. Classic evidence treats it as an only partly explained premium, not as evidence of a single mechanism \citep{BrownMedoff1989,OiIdson1999,troske1999evidence}. Developing-country studies show that the premium often survives stronger controls for worker heterogeneity, which weakens but does not eliminate a pure sorting interpretation \citep{Schaffner1998,Velenchik1997,SoderbomTealWambugu2005,reed2019large}. Matched employer--employee studies further show that both worker sorting and employer-associated pay components matter for wage dispersion \citep{AbowdKramarzMargolis1999,CardHeiningKline2013,Song2019,AlvarezBenguriaEngbomMoser2018}. In labor markets with large informal sectors, the interpretation is further complicated because firm size and formality are closely intertwined \citep{ElBadaouiStroblWalsh2010,GalianiWeinschelbaum2012,BalkanTumen2016}. The residual premium is therefore informative, but it is not by itself proof of productivity differences or allocative inefficiency. It is consistent with mechanisms that include firm-side pay differences, labor-market segmentation, unobserved worker sorting, or compensating job attributes; only some of these mechanisms would imply allocative frictions in the sense of the misallocation literature \citep{restuccia2008policy,hsieh2009misallocation}. 

Existing work for Colombia has studied wage differentials, informality, and firm characteristics, including evidence from the formal labor market \citep{iregui2012diferenciales}. Our contribution is narrower but distinct: we place firm size at the center of the wage analysis using nationally representative household data that include solo work, micro-firms, informal employment, and a long 2008--2025 period. This formality dimension is central because \citet{DazaGamboa2013} document persistent formal-informal wage gaps, and recent evidence on microfirms shows that business registration and labor-regulation compliance are associated with productivity differences \citep{GutierrezRodriguezLesmes2023}. These findings motivate our focus on the interaction between firm size and labor formality: the empirical question is not only whether larger firms pay more, but whether the size gradient remains once the formal-informal margin is made explicit.
